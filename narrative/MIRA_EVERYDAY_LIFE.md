# Mira — Everyday Life and Rooftop Scene

**Status:** Open concept / exploratory prose. Nothing in this document becomes canon unless the creator explicitly approves it.

## Purpose

Develop Mira as a person with a life of her own before deciding how the larger story is told. This is story-first work: prose and character truth come before any decision about a game, film, or other medium.

## Established foundations to preserve

- Mira Varek is Elias Varek's younger sister and was 17 when she disappeared.
- She is sociable, curious, hopeful, and wants a future beyond the lower city: to explore, make friends, become something in her own right, and perhaps one day have a family.
- Elias raised/protected her in practice. His protectiveness comes from fear of losing more people and from having little identity or reason to live beyond keeping her safe; it is not a lack of love.
- Mira understands why he protects her and often accepts that care, but she also keeps parts of her life private rather than constantly confronting him.
- Their home is in the inhabited lower districts of one enormous, physically connected, vertically layered city. The brighter upper districts are visible above; bridges, arches, and intermediate levels connect the city. The precise map and rules of access remain open.
- The parents' whereabouts and fate are unknown.
- The rooftop is an emotional anchor, but its exact placement in the opening and the larger chronology are not decided.
- The later argument, Elias choosing not to follow her, and Mira's disappearance remain a strongly favored working direction, not a fully written or locked sequence.

## Mira's private discoveries — working idea

Mira may have a habit of slipping into forgotten or overlooked places and collecting small objects: a shard of colored glass, a worn token, an unusual piece of metal, or something whose original purpose she cannot identify. The objects are not valuable in any obvious way. What matters is that she noticed them, imagined their history, and chose to keep them.

She does not announce this hobby to Elias or present it as a rebellion. It belongs to a private part of herself. If he notices an object, she might shrug and say, "Oh, I just found it." That answer can be truthful without being the whole truth.

**Why this may work:** It makes her curiosity physical and specific, creates opportunities for environmental storytelling, and gives her an inner life that exists beyond her brother. Avoid turning every object into a clue, prophecy, or plot device. Some things can matter only to Mira.

**Status:** Exploring, not canon.

## Rooftop scene — emotional intent

Mira leads Elias up to a high point she wants him to see. It should be a place they can plausibly reach from their lower district—not automatically the highest point in the city. The exact location, route, and access constraints remain open.

From there, they do not look down at a separate city. They see the same city they live in, extending upward through stone arches, bridges, roofs, and inhabited levels toward the brighter districts. The change is in perspective: familiar streets become part of a much larger place, and the heights seem to hold possibilities neither sibling can verify.

Mira asks whether their parents might be alive up there. The question is not proof that she believes they are alive, nor does it establish that the upper districts are safe or welcoming. It reveals that she is willing to imagine a future Elias has trained himself not to expect.

The scene should allow beauty to exist without immediately converting it into a speech about class, a simple rich-versus-poor contrast, or a promise that the upper city is better. Its emotional center is the siblings' different responses to uncertainty.

## Exploratory prose fragment

Mira led Elias up the worn steps without explaining why. At the top, the city did not end beneath them; it climbed away in every direction, roofs and bridges threaded between immense stone arches, familiar streets shrinking into a pattern, and the brighter heights rising beyond them.

For a while, Mira said nothing. She looked as if the view had given her more room to think.

"Do you think our parents are up there?"

Elias followed her gaze. He had learned to distrust questions that opened onto something he could not protect her from. Mira was looking at possibility. He could only see how far away it was.

*This fragment is a tone and emotional-direction test, not a finished scene. Details may change.*

## Perspective and characterization questions — deliberately open

- Whose perspective should carry the scene? Mira's would foreground curiosity and the future she imagines; Elias's would make the same view feel more threatening and reveal how vigilance shapes his attention. A later story may use both, but the scene should not switch viewpoint without a deliberate narrative reason.
- What small action shows Elias's fear before he says anything? It should be specific to him and not reduce him to a scowl or a lecture.
- What does Mira choose not to tell him that day? The answer should reveal a real boundary or desire, not make her mysteriously secretive for plot convenience.
- What makes this rooftop reachable, and why has Mira chosen it? Do not call it the highest accessible point until the city's vertical geography supports that decision.
- How does the moment connect chronologically to the later argument and disappearance? Keep the emotional memory consistent, but do not lock the scene into a prologue or flashback yet.

## Craft guardrails

- Show emotion through attention, physical choices, pauses, humor, and what the siblings do not say.
- Keep both characters loving and imperfect. Mira's desire for freedom should not make her reckless by default; Elias's fear should not make him controlling in every interaction.
- Do not make the upper districts automatically evil or the lower districts morally pure.
- The parents' fate, the city's political structure, Mira's ultimate fate, and the exact disappearance event remain unresolved.
- Keep this as open story development. Do not write gameplay, a script, or a production task list unless the creator explicitly asks to change mediums.
